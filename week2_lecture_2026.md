## SECTION 1 — HOW A MOVE HAPPENS (~20 min)

### S5 — Divider: Displacement = neighborhood change & migration **[W2-25 p.11]**
🎤 "Displacement is invisible in most data. What we can *see* is who moves, who can't, and what
the neighborhood looks like afterward. So we study displacement as **migration** plus
**neighborhood change**."

### S6 — The Mobility Curve (4-step build) **[W2-25 pp.12–15]**
- Desmond, Gershenson & Kiviat (2015), "Forced Relocation and Residential Instability among
  Urban Renters," *Social Service Review* 89(2):227–62 ✅ *(citation printed on slide)*
- U-shape: x = economic status, y = residential mobility
- Left third = **forced mobility** → your overlay: **Hard** (red) then **Soft** (orange)
- Middle = **immobility** — too poor to move
- Right = **unforced mobility** — moves by choice
🎤 "Same observable behavior — a household moves — three different social facts. At the top of
the income curve, moving is a choice: a better job, a bigger place. At the bottom, moving is
force: the sheriff or the rent. And in the middle is something we talk about less — people who
are *stuck*, who can't afford to leave. Poverty shows up as too much moving AND as no moving at
all. Keep this curve in your head all term: when we count moves in the Census, this is the
machine generating them."
- Discussion beat (60 sec): "Where has your household sat on this curve?" (no hands needed —
  rhetorical, or chat if it's flowing)

### S7 — Individual-Level Dynamics (the triangle) **[W2-25 p.16]**
- Increasing Rent + Housing Burden + Inadequate Welfare → **Hard & Soft Displacement**
- Desmond (2012), "Eviction and the Reproduction of Urban Poverty," *AJS* 118(1):88–133 ✅
🎤 "Three pressures, one household. Rent climbs, wages and benefits don't, and the share of
income going to housing swells until any shock — last week's 'internal causes' list — tips the
household off the ledge. Desmond's Milwaukee point: eviction isn't just a *result* of poverty,
it *reproduces* it."

### S8 — Milwaukee: Rent, Income & Welfare **[W2-25 p.17]**
- Desmond 2012 figure: 1-BR and 2-BR median rent climb 1997–2008; the welfare (W-2) grant is a
  flat line; minimum wage flat for years *(chart read)* ✅ *(source printed on slide)*
🎤 "This is the triangle as data. Rent rises every single year. The welfare grant never moves.
By the mid-2000s a two-bedroom eats most of a W-2 check before food or the bus. You don't need
a villain in this chart — the *lines crossing* is the eviction machine."

### S12 — Divider: Why do these patterns exist? **[W2-25 p.21]**

### S13 — Four Distinct Periods of Racial Inequity **[W2-25 p.22; highlight builds pp.23/27/35/42]**
- **Birth of the Ghetto** 1890–1940 = Migration & Exclusion
- **Collective Action Racism** 1940–1970 = Consolidation & Expansion

#### Period 2 — Collective Action Racism (1940–1970)

### S17 — Collective Action Racism **[W2-25 p.28]**
- Consolidation & expansion of the ghetto; Second Great Migration (1940–1970)
- Housing markets manipulated by law: **FHA loans** → creation of the suburbs & middle-class
  wealth — **Black families denied participation**
- **Restrictive housing covenants**; **blockbusting** (realtors inducing fear of Black
  in-migration); overt intimidation
- Massey & Denton (1993), *American Apartheid* — the creation of the underclass ✅ *(on slide)*
🎤 "Period two: exclusion goes from improvised to *organized*. The federal government
mass-produces the white middle class — thirty-year FHA mortgages, new suburbs — and locks Black
families out of the program. Meanwhile covenants and blockbusting police the city line by line.
'Collective action' because it took everyone: the FHA, the realtor, the neighborhood
association, the mob."

### S18 — Restrictive Housing Covenants **[W2-25 p.29]**
- Innis Arden ("A Restricted Residential Community"): *"No property in said addition shall at
  any time be sold, conveyed, rented or leased in whole or in part to any person or persons not
  of the White or Caucasian race…"* — domestic servants excepted *(deed text on slide)*
- **Over 400 restrictive covenants in King County alone** (Seattle Civil Rights & Labor History
  Project) ✅ *(on slide)*
- ADD ✅: *Shelley v. Kraemer*, 334 U.S. 1 (**1948**) — courts can't *enforce* racial covenants
  (14th Amendment). But the covenants stayed on the deeds, private compliance continued, and
  discrimination in sales stayed legal until 1968.
🎤 "Read clause 14. Innis Arden is a real neighborhood — Boeing-adjacent, still there. Four
hundred–plus of these in King County alone. In 1948 the Supreme Court said courts couldn't
enforce them — it did *not* make them disappear; they sat in deeds as instructions people kept
following for another twenty years. Some of you will find covenant language in your own county's
records."

#### Period 3 — Decentralized Racism (1970–1990)

### S22 — Decentralized Racism **[W2-25 p.36]**
- Civil Rights Act (**Fair Housing Act**) **1968** — dismantles *legal* discrimination ✅
- Segregation peaks ~1970–80, then starts declining *(on slide)*
- But: lasting legacy of redlining, blockbusting, discrimination → little opportunity to move
  to better neighborhoods; **concentrated poverty keeps rising**
- Black middle class → suburbs; white flight & avoidance; Latinx growth
🎤 "Nineteen sixty-eight: the law changes. The machine doesn't stop — it *decentralizes*. No
more federal maps or enforceable covenants; instead a million private decisions — steering,
lending, white flight, 'avoidance.' Segregation slowly declines on the index, while concentrated
poverty *deepens*. And notice the trap: the language is now race-neutral. From here on, nobody
has to *say* race to reproduce it. That's why we need data."

#### Period 4 — Urban Revitalization (1990–2019)
